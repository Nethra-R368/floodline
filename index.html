        About FloodLine

        When Chennai floods, phone lines go down and people ask for rescue on WhatsApp, X and Instagram. Volunteer groups read those messages by hand and pass them to boat teams. It works, but the same few things go wrong every time:

        • One family gets posted ten times by different relatives, and two boats go to the same house.
        • Nobody knows which requests are already handled, so old requests keep circulating after the family is safe.
        • A request nobody picked up scrolls out of view, and nobody notices.

        FloodLine is a shared list for that job. Volunteers paste the messages in. It pulls out the area, phone number, number of people, water level and who is vulnerable; merges repeats of the same household; puts the most urgent at the top with the reasons written out; and keeps each request on the list until someone marks the family as rescued.

        Tested on a real flood

        The Real flood test tab plays 520 real rescue posts from the night of 2–3 December 2015 in Chennai — exactly as people wrote them, at the minute they were posted, with names and numbers changed. FloodLine turned them into a list of 247 households, caught over 200 reposts of families already on the list, and put 49 critical households (women in labour, babies, people needing medical care) at the top. On 60 of those posts labelled by hand, Gemini in the cloud with FloodLine's checks read 94.7% of the details right.

        Who it is for

        • Volunteer control rooms — the 5–50 people who, in every Chennai flood, sit with phones and laptops reading the requests that pour into WhatsApp groups and social media, and pass them to boat teams.
        • Resident welfare associations and NGOs that run their own rescue and relief in one area.
        • Officials who receive forwarded lists from volunteers and need them without repeats and in order of urgency.

        How a group starts using it

        1. Open the site once on each laptop before the monsoon (it then works offline), and press "Prepare" in Settings to download Gemini Nano.
        2. When a flood starts, switch to the live board and paste messages in as they arrive — one at a time or a whole exported WhatsApp chat.
        3. Take a request, call or WhatsApp the family from the request, mark it rescued. At the end of a shift, download the board locked with a passphrase and hand it to the next shift.

        It has been done before

        In the 2018 Kerala floods, volunteers built keralarescue.in, an open-source site for rescue requests that the state adopted as its official one. FloodLine is a much smaller attempt at the same idea for Chennai, built for the way requests actually arrive here — as forwarded WhatsApp messages in Tamil, English and Tanglish.

        How it decides what is urgent

        Points, not a black box: pregnant or in labour +40, needs medical care +40, neck-deep water +35, baby +25, elderly +20, cannot walk +20, stuck on a terrace +20, plus time waiting with no one assigned and how many times it was reported. Every request shows its points. A volunteer can always overrule the order.

        Where the AI is used, and where it isn't

        Messages are first read by plain rules that work offline and never make things up. Then Gemini re-reads each message to catch what rules miss ("near Phoenix mall" is Velachery; "old couple" is two people). Where Chrome has it, this happens with Gemini Nano on the device itself: it works with no internet, which is exactly when floods knock networks out, and the message never leaves the device. Otherwise Gemini runs in the cloud. Both go through Firebase AI Logic. Anything Gemini returns is checked against the original message: a phone number that isn't in the text, an area that isn't on the map or an address nobody wrote is thrown away, and the request says so.

        What it can't do yet

        • Boards sync between windows on one device. Different phones need a small server, which isn't built yet.
        • On-device AI needs desktop Chrome with Gemini Nano; elsewhere Gemini runs in the cloud, which needs internet. Without either, rules still read every message.
        • It knows 54 Chennai localities (28 flood-prone ones plus 26 the real 2015 posts kept naming). Anything else shows as "area not recognised".
        • Pins are at the locality, not the house.
        • It reads text only — not voice notes or photos.
        • It has been tested on real posts from the 2015 flood, but not yet used live by a volunteer group during a flood. The boat comparison on the Results page is a simulation.

        What comes next

        • One board across phones: Firebase Firestore with sign-in for a group, keeping the same event log, hash chain and end-to-end-locked handover.
        • WhatsApp intake: forward a message to a FloodLine number instead of pasting it.
        • Voice notes: many requests arrive as Tamil voice notes; Gemini can transcribe them on the device.
        • A pilot with a Chennai volunteer group before the next north-east monsoon.

        Privacy

        There is no FloodLine server. Messages stay in the volunteer's browser. When Gemini Nano reads a message on the device, nothing is sent anywhere. When Gemini reads it in the cloud, the message text goes to Google's Gemini API through Firebase, and nowhere else.

        One board on every volunteer's phone

        Works on the live board. Create a group code and read it out to the other volunteers; everyone who enters it sees the same board, live. Every message and action is encrypted on this device before it is sent (AES-256-GCM, key made from the code), so the server only ever stores ciphertext. Changes made offline are sent when the network is back.

        Handing over to the next shift

        Download everything on this board — every message and every action — as one file, and load it on another device. The file holds families' phone numbers, so lock it with a passphrase you tell the next shift in person or on a call (AES-256-GCM, key from PBKDF2-SHA-256, 310,000 rounds; it never leaves this device).
